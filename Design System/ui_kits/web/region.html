The Land & Its Soul

A geography of myth,
carved by glaciers and kings.

Bavaria is not one place but seven, each carrying its own dialect, its own cuisine, its own stubborn idea of what it means to be Bavarian. The Franks in the north will tell you their wine is better than any beer in Munich. The Swabians in the west hold fast to their potato dumplings and their reputation for thrift. The Upper Bavarians, nearest the Alps, claim the mountains and the lederhosen and the world's image of the place.

All of them are right. None of them are complete. To travel through Bavaria is to travel through a landscape that refuses to be flattened into a single postcard.

Bavaria is a kind of paradise. If you can't be happy here, you can't be happy anywhere.— Thomas Mann, 1919

And then there are the castles. Seventy-some of them, depending on how you count, but one above all: Neuschwanstein, the fairy-tale citadel perched on its crag, built by a king who drowned in a lake under circumstances no one has ever fully explained.

On Ludwig II Known as der Märchenkönig — the Fairy-Tale King — Ludwig nearly bankrupted the Bavarian throne building three castles he would barely live to see completed.

A History Written in Stone

Bavaria's history spans more than a thousand years — from the founding of the Duchy of Bavaria in 1180 by Otto I of Wittelsbach, whose dynasty would rule uninterrupted until 1918, through the Thirty Years' War devastation, the rise of the Kingdom of Bavaria under Napoleon's reorganisation of Europe, and the peculiar tragedy of Ludwig II, who bankrupted a treasury building monuments to his own imagination.

The Wittelsbachs were, by most accounts, an eccentric dynasty. They produced saints and madmen in roughly equal proportion. Ludwig I was forced to abdicate over an affair with the dancer Lola Montez. His grandson Ludwig II preferred the company of swans to statesmen.

Getting to Bavaria

By Air: Munich Airport (MUC) is the main gateway, 40 minutes from the city centre by S-Bahn. Nuremberg Airport serves the north. Direct flights connect Munich to most European capitals and major international hubs.

By Rail: Munich Hauptbahnhof is one of Germany's busiest stations. ICE trains connect to Frankfurt (3h), Berlin (4h), Vienna (4h). The Bavarian rail network is excellent — regional trains reach most towns within 2–3 hours.

By Road: The Autobahn network is comprehensive. Note that some sections have no speed limit, but urban areas and construction zones are strictly enforced.

Beyond Bavaria's Borders

Bavaria's neighbours reward extension. Austria's Tyrol begins where the Alps continue — Innsbruck is 2 hours from Munich. The Czech Republic's Bohemian Forest lies directly east, sharing the same geological ridge as the Bavarian Forest. Salzburg, technically Austrian but culturally indistinguishable from Bavaria in many respects, sits 90 minutes from Munich.

When to Come

Spring (Apr–May): Wildflowers in the Alpine foothills, moderate crowds, occasional snow above 1,000m. The best light for photography.

Summer (Jun–Aug): Peak season. Neuschwanstein queues begin at 7am. Lakes reach swimming temperature. The beer gardens open fully in Munich.

Autumn (Sep–Oct): Oktoberfest (late Sep). Forests turn gold. Fewer tourists outside Munich. The best time for the Romantic Road.

Winter (Nov–Mar): Christmas markets in Nuremberg and Munich are among Germany's finest. Skiing in Garmisch from December. Some mountain roads close.
