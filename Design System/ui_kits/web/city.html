Neighbourhoods

Five quarters, five stories.

The Altstadt is Munich's original heart — a grid of medieval lanes whose width was determined by the turning radius of a loaded wagon. At its centre, Marienplatz has functioned as market, execution ground, and gathering point for nine centuries. The Glockenspiel still performs daily at 11am, though what it celebrates is mainly itself.

Marienplatz
Munich's central square since 1158, anchored by the neo-Gothic New Town Hall.
Viktualienmarkt
A daily open-air market trading in sausages, cheese, and the particular rudeness that Munich considers hospitality.
Hofbräuhaus
The world's most famous beer hall, built in 1589. Today it serves 10,000 litres daily.

Schwabing was Munich's bohemian quarter — the neighbourhood where Thomas Mann wrote, where Kandinsky painted, where Lenin lived briefly before returning to Russia. Today it is expensive and respectable, but the cafés still remember their former inhabitants.

Englischer Garten
Larger than Central Park. Nude sunbathers, a Japanese tea house, and surfers on the Eisbach wave.
Leopoldstrasse
The boulevard of Schwabing — boutiques, cafés, and the Siegestor triumphal arch at its southern end.
Wedekindplatz
A square named for the playwright Frank Wedekind, surrounded by late-19th-century tenements.

Maxvorstadt is Munich's museum district — three of Germany's finest art museums within ten minutes' walk of each other. Ludwig I built it as a cultural statement, lining the Königsplatz with neoclassical temples to knowledge and beauty. The Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität dominates its eastern edge.

Deutsches Museum
The world's largest science and technology museum, with 73,000 objects on display across 28 departments.
Alte Pinakothek
European paintings from the 14th to 18th centuries. One of the world's oldest public galleries.
Königsplatz
A neoclassical square built to Ludwig I's specifications, modelled on the Acropolis of Athens.

Haidhausen — east of the Isar — was Munich's working-class quarter until the 1980s, when artists and students arrived seeking cheap rents. The rents are no longer cheap, but the neighbourhood retains its particular mix of old beer halls and organic bakeries.

Gasteig
Munich's cultural centre, home to the Philharmoniker and the city library.
Wiener Platz
A neighbourhood market square with a permanent food market and Austrian-inflected cafés.
Müllersches Volksbad
A Jugendstil public baths complex from 1901, still operating as a swimming pool.

Giesing is the Munich that postcards ignore — a southern working neighbourhood without tourist infrastructure. It is honest and unpolished, and for exactly this reason it is becoming rapidly fashionable.

Giesinger Bräu
A microbrewery in a former industrial building, serving unfiltered lagers to a loyal local following.
Heilig-Kreuz-Kirche
A baroque church on a hill, with views over southern Munich's rooftops.
Perlach Forest
A municipal forest beginning at Giesing's southern edge — a reminder that Munich was always a city beside a wilderness.

Practical Information

What the guidebooks get right.

Munich's MVV network integrates U-Bahn (subway), S-Bahn (suburban rail), trams, and buses under a single ticket system. A day pass (€9.20) covers unlimited travel within the inner zone, which includes all major sights. The S-Bahn connects the airport to the central station in 40 minutes. Tram 17 reaches Nymphenburg; the U6 connects the Olympiapark area to the city centre.

Cycling is excellent. The city has 1,200km of dedicated cycle lanes and a public bike-share scheme (MVG Rad) with stations throughout.

Altstadt-Lehel: Central and expensive. Walking distance from Marienplatz, the museums, and the Englischer Garten. For travellers who want to be within ten minutes of everything.

Schwabing: More residential, with better cafés and lower prices. A 20-minute walk or two-stop U-Bahn ride to the centre.

Haidhausen: East of the Isar. Unpretentious, good restaurants, slightly cheaper. Fifteen minutes by U4/U5 to Marienplatz.

Munich takes its food seriously. The Viktualienmarkt has operated since 1807 and is the best place to understand what the city eats. Beer gardens open from April; the Biergarten in the Englischer Garten serves 7,000 guests. The Hofbräuhaus is mandatory but crowded; for quieter beer hall experience, try the Augustiner-Keller in Maxvorstadt.

For Bavarian food without tourist markup: seek the neighbourhood Wirtshäuser (inns) in Haidhausen or Giesing.

Neuschwanstein Castle: 2 hours by train to Füssen, then bus or taxi. Book tickets 2 days in advance. Arrive early — the castle opens at 9am but queues form at 8.

Zugspitze: 1.5 hours to Garmisch by regional train, then cogwheel railway or cable car to the summit. Germany's highest point at 2,962m.

Salzburg: 90 minutes by regional train. Mozart's birthplace, the Hohensalzburg fortress, and the Altstadt's baroque churches.
